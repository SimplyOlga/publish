 He was sitting at a corner booth in an Italian restaurant while Anubis went to check on his appearance in the restroom. For a moment, Noah contemplated making a run for it, but who could outrun death? Especially in a state like his where even holding a menu was a little challenging. 

“Made up your mind?”

Out of spite, Noah pointed at the most expensive dishes, fully aware he would not be able to finish them all. Still, the god nodded, taking his menu and walking to make an order at the counter. The generosity was making him uncomfortable, but Noah was intending to take the most out of the evening: both the food and the opportunity to talk in a public space where causing a scene could be to his advantage. 

Unless Anubis would just stop his heart once again. 

“They promised to be quick.”

Noah dismissed the hints of remorse in the god’s voice, tapping at the clean white tablecloth with a breadstick. 

The silence was awkward, and realising he had no reasons to fear making it even more so, Noah stared openly at the hound’s human disguise.

The man looked ambiguous in his age where you could say him to be anywhere between 30 and 50, a confusing age range. The dark hair was lightly dusted with gray of age, and the wrinkles on his eyes were less so from smiling too much, and more so due to frowning just like he was doing at the moment.  

Together, especially then, they made for an odd pair to stumble into a diner on a random weekday. Suddenly, Noah was anxiously aware of his dishevelled unkempt looks. 

“And here you go, your appetisers. The main course will be here shortly, have a lovely meal.” 

Noah stared at the bruschetta and swallowed hard: his starvation was making him only feel sick at the image and smell of food in front of him. Still, the god nudged him: 

“I made an error in my judgement out of forgetfulness. Please, consider my ways fixed, I will take better care to tend to your primary human needs.”

Noah did not need this hand out, neither did he need any “care of his human needs”. Still, he picked a piece of bread with a smile, eyes staring into Anubis’ with a silent provocation. 

With every passing moment he was more confident in his plan, and more confident in weaknesses the god of death thought he wouldn’t notice. 

“Appreciated.”

He savoured the taste of warm bread and tomatoes and looked at the way the hound started at his salad. The idea of watching a god eat was amusing. 

“Are you now going to apologise?”


The fork in the god’s hand slid against the plate with a loud unpleasant scratch. Anubis looked up at Noah who only chewed his meal slowly, afraid to throw up.

“Come on now.”

The god’s hand clenched around the cutlery at the audacity, still he took a deep breath and went along:

“I apologise for my forgetfulness. Like I just said, consider my ways f-”

“And?”

The deity groaned.

“I am sorry I… took your heart out without your consent.”

It was absolutely normal for him, though, so he really thought the situation to be incredibly stupid and Noah-centered. Still, he diligently complied until Noah overstepped the last boundary:

“What about apologising to Leo?”

Anubis was really, really tired. He put the cutlery down and opened his mouth only to be silenced by a full tray of food his human companion ordered arriving.

“Enjoy your dinner.”

Not for a moment did Noah take his eyes off Anubis, waiting, signaling that there will be no peace treaty unless it's by his rules. 

There was clearly a weakness in the god that was not hard to spot: a weakness for being wrong. In a way, as Noah was his biggest mistake, that also made him the god’s biggest weakness. 

And Noah intended to use that to its fullest. 

“There is something I am curious about,” he started, seeing the god relax a little at the prospect of topic changing. “Why was I sent to you?”

“Because you died.”

“No, what I mean is,” he wondered if that would be a sensitive topic. In a way he hoped it would be, proceeding with a lightly devilish intent. “There are other gods in history associated with death, why was I sent to you? Do the others exist?”

Anubis thought for a moment, likely deciding how much was safe to say. 

“There are.”

“So why was I sent to you? I mean, I am English, I’ve never even been to Egypt.” Noah’s eyes squinted. “Is this a payback for the British museum? I had nothing to do with that though-”

“It isn’t like that.”

Anubis rested his head on the table, eyes doing down like he was digging deep into something half personal. 

“I am the only god of death left.”

Noah felt like the words were a verdict, a wound he just carelessly opened. A wound deep enough to make him regret his own stupid remarks. Sensing unease in his companion, the hound continued:

“They passed naturally, leaving behind the eldest. It is a perfectly normal sequence of events.”

Still, Noah felt weird about it. 

“I am sorry.”

“There is no need to apologise for something so out of your control. You were sent to me because you are my responsibility.”

So truly, they were destined to meet. After all, one can not avoid death forever. 

Noah felt his appetite drop, but considering it unwise and impolite to stop there, he still pushed the expensive meal down his throat. 

“There are other gods, if that’s what interests you.”

Noah perked up.

“Like who?” He tried to recall names from his middle school reading program. “Zeus? Is Zeus real?”

Anubis chuckled. 

“He used to be, yes.”

Noah bit at his tongue, cursing his insensitiveness. 

“What exactly happens when a god... dies?”

Anubis pushed another dish closer to him, forcing some salad onto Noah’s plate. 

“When humanity was young and naive, they needed comfort,” the god spoke with a tone of an elder telling a story to a child, so Noah listened quietly. “We are born out of fear and curiosity, we are the personification of humanity’s desire for understanding.”

“Back when the sky was a dome and thunder was an omen of unspeakable bad will, humanity gave faces and names to the powers beyond their understanding. They needed a way to address them to appease them, so they were given titles. They needed a way to soothe their wrath, so shrines were erected and humanoid features were added for simplicity of connection. A human needs another human to feel safer: so we, gods, received mouths to eat what’s offered and eyes to watch over those who pray.” 

Noah thought it made sense, thinking back to how he as a child would hide under the blankets from the roar of thunder he thought to be a monster coming for him. A creature so powerful it shook the sky just to scare him. 

“As the time passed, the need for such comfort lowered,” the god continued, tracing shapes on the table surface with his fingers, absentmindedly, stuck in old memories. “The piercing thunder arrows of Zeus became charges of electricity, the golden chariot that passes through the sky became a celestial body in the grand cosmos. The old gods died, leaving the podium to the new divine: science, research, understanding.”

“So why are you still here? Not to be rude or anything, but…”

“Because there still are things that science explains in a way that doesn’t grant comfort - it only makes people seek it elsewhere.” 

Noah thought back to the many funerals he had to attend in his life. The tears, the prayers, the words of comfort. Death was an end to live, simple as that, and yet the most complicated fact to come to terms with. 

“Death is simple, and yet complicated,” the god agreed with Noah’s unspoken sorrow flashing through his eyes. “One of us had to stay behind to guide the souls to their end.”

“But what is there, in the end?”

Anubis smiled, fondly, as if it was the very question he expected. 

“There is more. That’s all I can tell you, for that is all I know.”

Noah had seen many people pass in his life, leaving behind photographs and memories, making him wonder: wouldn’t they want them? In there, where they were going, would they not want to hold on to those?

He thought of himself, unable to die, lingering in-between the worlds under the watchful eyes of Death itself. 

What would he himself want the other side to be like?

“Apologies for spoiling your meal with work talk,” Anubis nudged him to continue and only then did Noah realise he had been sitting still for a while.

‘Right,’ he thought. ‘To you it’s work talk.’

“Are there any others who are left, among the gods?”

“Yes, a few. We barely talk.”

Somehow Noah saw it coming, so he grinned. 

“Someone’s not being very friendly to his colleagues.”

He teased, just a little, realising even a god could feel a sense of loss, loneliness. Especially the god of Death. 

Anubis smiled, making it seem like just a casual little dinner between two friends, pushing a small bowl of olives towards the human.

“You want to ask me for something.”

“You know what it is.”

“I could… grant you an audience with him.”

Noah’s hands trembled, he didn’t expect Anubis to propose it himself. 

“I can’t promise you anything else, but you have to understand some things just need to be done. Death is inevitable.” 

Noah nodded. He had outlived quite a few people in his life, and thinking back to the apathy and acceptance he had felt on every funeral so far he wondered what brought up this unexpected resistance to the natural. Was it simply that he could? Has his own unnatural immortality caused this belief that a mistake could become a pattern? 

He felt apologetic for the very thought. His situation was already causing a stir and the god in front of him would want nothing less than for it to repeat. 

“I understand.”

Anubis fiddled with his wine glass before chugging it down. 

“Is it time to leave already?” Noah was full plenty, but the idea of leaving the outer world so soon was making him melancholic. 

“We don’t have to go yet. Take your time.”

And so he did. 

They talked for a while about pointless things that shouldn’t even be noticed by the age-old immortal, and yet Anubis listened, patiently, with interest. They talked about anything but the things that mattered, nothing about themselves or their lives, just everything that would pass more for a small talk was it not hours deep into the night. 

By the time Noah’s eyes were closing, the god requested some takeaway and finally took them back. 

“I want to see him.”

“It is late, you sho-“

“You promised me, Anubis.”

At the name the god hissed but didn’t correct him, pausing at his door before reluctantly agreeing. 

They walked through the corridors that all looked the same, and yet Noah mapped them all in his mind, in hope. By the time they stopped by a metal door, he was impatient, reaching out to open it just before a hand stopped him: 

“I will give you one last warning. Your friend is not who you might think he is. So please, take what you see as the truth, from a new unbiased perspective.”

That was not a direct threat, and yet Anubis hoped it would put doubt into the man’s heart. Watching Noah open the door with no less resolve, he stepped back and let him in. 

Noah took a step and froze. 

Leo turned to look at him and as their eyes met he smiled:

“Hello, Noah.”

Noah took in the sight, feeling his stomach turning. 

It was no longer just a cell: glass bottles and contraptions occupied most of it, leaving only space for a wide metal table in the middle. And on it was a body. A moving, breathing, speaking body with a gaping hole where his flesh was cut open to reveal- 

Nothing. 

An empty torso with nothing but meat and bones that barely even aligned. 

Leo chuckled. 

“You look better than me, pal.”

Feeling himself retch, Noah was no longer able to hold it in.
